# Wind Is the Weather {#ch-6}

<!-- 6.1 origin: author (diary-2020-05) -->
<!-- author-verbatim -->
The storm came on a Tuesday in May 2020. I had planted out twelve 'Little Gem' lettuces the weekend before, proud of their tight green heads. By Wednesday morning they were lying flat against the potting mix, leaves torn and grey at the edges, as if someone had ironed them. Nothing had frozen. It had simply blown for fourteen hours.

That was the day I stopped checking the temperature first.
<!-- /author-verbatim -->

## Why wind matters more than cold {#sec-6-2}

<!-- 6.2 origin: ai-draft-rewritten (author 64%) -->
On a balcony, wind does three kinds of damage, and only one of them is obvious.

The obvious one is physical: torn leaves, snapped stems, flowers stripped before they set fruit, and containers that tip and roll. The second is drying. Moving air pulls water out of leaves faster than roots in a small container can replace it, so a windy day in May can wilt plants that would shrug off the same temperature in still air [S7]. The third is slow: plants that are shaken all day put their energy into staying upright and grow short and tough instead of leafy.

In my experience the drying is the one that catches people out. The potting mix looks damp in the morning; by three in the afternoon the lettuce is limp.

## Height and corners {#sec-6-3}

<!-- 6.3 origin: ai-draft-rewritten (author 77%) -->
Wind is generally stronger the higher you go, and it speeds up where it is squeezed around the edge of a building [S9]. If your balcony sits on a corner, like mine on the seventh floor, you are getting both.

![A plan view of a rectangular building with wind arriving from the left. Marta's terrace, three floors up and set back from the edge, gets a fraction of the gusts I do, even though we have had the same forecasts on the same days.

## A windbreak that breathes {#sec-6-4}

<!-- 6.4 origin: ai-draft-rewritten (author 52%) -->
The instinct is to build a wall. Resist it. A solid screen stops the wind directly behind it, but the air pours over the top and tumbles down on the other side, so the plants a little way back get buffeted harder than before. A screen that lets roughly half the air through slows the wind over a longer distance without that tumbling effect [S8].

![Two side-view diagrams. Top: a solid screen with wind lines curling over it and swirling down close behind, marked "turbulence". Bottom: a slatted screen about half open, with wind lines passing through it and staying smooth and slower for a longer distance, marked "sheltered zone".](art/fig-6-2.svg){#fig-6-2}

Figure 6.2. A solid screen (top) creates turbulence; a semi-permeable screen (bottom) shelters further.

Willow hurdles, slatted wooden panels and windbreak netting all work. On a rented balcony, fix them with straps or clamps to the railing rather than screws, and check the rules first (see chapter 2) [S4] [S5].

::: {.checklist}
**Before the first May storm**

- Move tall containers to the most sheltered corner.
- Strap or clamp the windbreak; check every fixing.
- Water in the evening before a windy forecast.
- Put saucers away: they become flying objects.
:::
